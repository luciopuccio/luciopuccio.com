3. Chapter three · the idea

Wrap any circle in string. It's always a bit more than three.

Take a coin, a bike wheel or the Moon: measure around the edge, divide by the distance across, and you get the same answer every time — 3.14159… That ratio is π. Its decimals never end and never settle into a repeating pattern (Johann Lambert proved it irrational in 1761). In 1882 Ferdinand von Lindemann went further and showed it's transcendental — which finally proved the ancient puzzle of "squaring the circle" with ruler and compass is impossible.

1 Chapter one · the chase

Four thousand years of chasing a never-ending number.

Every civilisation that built with circles wanted π. Here's how the hunt unfolded — from clay tablets to supercomputers.

c. 1900 BC

Babylon: 3.125

A clay tablet from Susa uses 25⁄8 for the circle ratio — already within half a percent.

c. 1650 BC

Egypt: 3.16

The Rhind Papyrus finds a circle's area by squaring 8⁄9 of its diameter — π of about 3.16.

c. 250 BC

Archimedes traps π

Squeezing the circle between two 96-sided polygons, he proves π lies between 223⁄71 and 22⁄7.

AD 263 – 480

China: 355⁄113

Liu Hui refines polygons; Zu Chongzhi then finds 355⁄113 — correct to six decimals and unbeaten for around 900 years.

c. 1400

India: infinite series

Madhava of the Kerala school writes π as an endless sum — centuries before Europe discovers the same idea.

1706 – 1737

π gets its name

Welsh mathematician William Jones first uses the Greek letter; Leonhard Euler makes it famous.

1949

Enter the computer

ENIAC crunches 2,037 digits in about 70 hours — the first record set by an electronic machine.

2025 – 2026

314 trillion digits

A single server finishes the run in November 2025; Guinness certifies it in June 2026. The chase continues.

4 Chapter four · building & measuring

Anything that turns, curves or circles was built with π.

The Pantheon's perfect sphere

Rome's great dome is 43.3 m across and exactly as high — a whole sphere would fit inside. Nearly 1,900 years on it's still the largest unreinforced concrete dome on Earth, and every curve of it is geometry built on π.

Wheels, gears & engines

Each turn of a wheel moves you π × its diameter. Your car's speedometer, a bike computer and every gearbox count on that simple fact.

The surveyor's wheel

Roll a measuring wheel along a road and it clicks once per circumference. Distance, measured by π, one click at a time.

Arches, tunnels & pipes

How much brick for an arch, steel for a tunnel ring or water through a pipe — engineers reach for π first.

GPS & satellites

Orbits are ellipses and circles. Plotting where a satellite is — and so where your phone is — runs on π.

1 Chapter one (again) · nature & science

π turns up in places that have nothing to do with circles.

Waves

Sound, light & the sea

Every wave repeats in cycles of 2π. Music, radio, Wi-Fi and ocean swell are all described with it.

Rivers

The meandering π

In 1996 geologist Hans-Henrik Stølum suggested winding rivers average about π times longer than their straight-line distance. Lovely idea — still hotly debated.

Chance

The bell curve

Heights, exam scores, measurement errors — the normal distribution that describes them has √(2π) baked into its formula.

Needles

Buffon's needle

Drop needles on striped floorboards and count how many cross a line: the odds slowly reveal π. Pure randomness, circular answer.

The cosmos

Einstein & quantum

Gravity in general relativity carries an 8π; quantum physics divides Planck's constant by 2π. The universe's rulebook is full of it.

The most beautiful equation

eiπ + 1 = 0

Euler's identity ties together five of maths' most important numbers in one tiny line.

5 Chapter five · culture & fun

The only number with its own holiday (and a law that nearly broke it).

3/14
14 MARCH

Pi Day

Physicist Larry Shaw threw the first Pi Day party at San Francisco's Exploratorium in 1988. Today UNESCO marks 14 March as the International Day of Mathematics. Bonus: it's Einstein's birthday. And 22 July (22⁄7) is Pi Approximation Day.

The Indiana Pi Bill

In 1897 Indiana's lawmakers almost voted a flawed circle-squaring "proof" into law, implying π = 3.2. A visiting maths professor talked the Senate out of it just in time.

The Feynman point

At the 762nd decimal, π suddenly shows six 9s in a row. Physicist Richard Feynman joked he'd memorise up to there and finish with "…nine, nine, and so on".

Piphilology

The art of remembering π with words whose letter counts spell its digits. A classic opener: "How I want a drink…" — 3, 1, 4, 1, 5.

And yes — Life of Pi

Yann Martel's hero, Piscine Patel, renames himself after the number and chalks 3.14 on the board so nobody can mock him again. It's where this page began.
